## 1. Overview and intended use

TUG//LAB times the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test from a single smartphone camera,
automatically segments the trial into its constituent phases, and derives a
detailed set of spatiotemporal gait and whole-body biomechanical measures using
**on-device markerless pose estimation**. No video or raw landmark data leaves
the device; only pseudonymous, numeric summary data is optionally transmitted to
a Google Sheet for aggregation.

**Intended users.** Allied-health and sports-science practitioners, researchers,
and educators conducting mobility and fall-risk screening in community-dwelling
older adults and comparable populations.

**Intended setting.** Clinic rooms, laboratories, community centres, and field
settings with a clear 3 m walkway and adequate lighting.

**Not intended** for unsupervised home use by at-risk individuals, for diagnosis,
or as a sole determinant of any clinical decision.

**Design philosophy.** Two decisions shape the whole system:

1. **Zero-installation.** The app is static HTML/CSS/JavaScript. There is no Node,
   npm, bundler, Git client, or Python anywhere in the authoring, testing,
   deployment, or data-capture path. Pose estimation loads from a pinned CDN;
   unit tests run in a browser page; the data dictionary is generated by a
   browser page; deployment is a drag-and-drop upload to GitHub; cloud capture
   uses Google Apps Script.
2. **Operator-following portrait camera.** Rather than a fixed, wide, landscape
   camera framing the entire course, a single operator holds the phone in
   portrait and pans to follow the participant. This is ergonomic and
   space-efficient, but it invalidates the usual "image axis = walking axis"
   assumption; §7 explains how the analysis remains valid using metric world
   landmarks.

## 2. The Timed Up and Go test: clinical science

### 2.1 Origin and construct
The TUG was introduced by Podsiadlo and Richardson (1991) as a timed,
standardised evolution of the earlier Get-Up and Go test (Mathias et al., 1986).
It measures **basic functional mobility**: the integrated ability to rise from a
chair, walk a short distance, turn, return, and sit — a sequence that recruits
lower-limb strength, dynamic balance, gait, and motor planning. Because it
condenses several everyday transfers into one brief, observable task, it has
become one of the most widely used mobility measures in geriatrics,
neurology, and rehabilitation (Barry et al., 2014).

### 2.2 Standardised protocol (CDC STEADI)
TUG//LAB implements the CDC STEADI protocol (CDC, 2017):

- **Equipment.** A standard **armchair** (seat height ≈ 46 cm) that is stable and
  **not placed against a wall**; a floor marker or cone **3 m (10 ft)** from the
  front edge of the chair; a timing device (here, the application).
- **Starting position.** The participant sits with their back against the
  backrest, wearing their usual footwear, and may use a usual walking aid.
- **Instruction.** "When I say *Go*, stand up, walk to the line at your normal
  pace, turn around, walk back to the chair, and sit down."
- **Timing.** Timing begins on the command **"Go"** and stops when the
  participant's buttocks contact the seat on return.
- **Practice.** One untimed practice trial is recommended to familiarise the
  participant.
- **Supervision.** The assessor remains beside the participant throughout for
  safety.

> *Content in this section has been paraphrased and condensed from the primary
> sources for licensing compliance; consult the cited works for verbatim
> protocol wording.*

### 2.3 Why automate it with computer vision
Manual stopwatch timing captures a single scalar (total time) and relies on
operator reaction time, which introduces systematic and random error.
Markerless pose estimation additionally yields **phase-resolved** timing (how
long each sub-task took) and **spatiotemporal gait parameters** (stride length,
cadence, symmetry, variability), which are themselves independent predictors of
adverse outcomes such as falls and functional decline (Verghese et al., 2009;
Hausdorff et al., 2001). Capturing these at the point of care, without markers or
wearable sensors, lowers the barrier to richer, more objective assessment.

## 3. Psychometrics: reliability, validity, responsiveness

- **Inter- and intra-rater reliability.** The TUG shows excellent reliability in
  older adults and in several clinical populations, with intraclass correlation
  coefficients commonly reported in the 0.9+ range for community-dwelling elders
  (Podsiadlo & Richardson, 1991; Shumway-Cook et al., 2000).
- **Construct and concurrent validity.** TUG time correlates with the Berg
  Balance Scale, gait speed, and functional independence measures
  (Podsiadlo & Richardson, 1991; Shumway-Cook et al., 2000).
- **Predictive validity for falls.** Evidence is **population-dependent**. A
  systematic review and meta-analysis found the TUG has limited ability to
  discriminate future fallers from non-fallers in unselected community-dwelling
  older adults, and cautioned against using it as a sole screening test
  (Barry et al., 2014). It performs better in higher-risk and clinical cohorts.
  TUG//LAB therefore reports the CDC cut-point as a **screening flag**, not a
  diagnostic probability, and does not reproduce the opaque server-side
  machine-learning fall probability of the predecessor system.
- **Responsiveness / minimal detectable change (MDC).** Clinically meaningful
  change thresholds vary by population; practitioners should apply
  population-specific MDC values from the literature when interpreting repeat
  tests, rather than treating small time differences as real change.

## 5. Biomechanics of the TUG sub-tasks

The app decomposes each trial into eight events and the phases between them, and
reports mechanics for each. All geometry is derived from **metric 3-D world
landmarks** (§6), so joint angles and body-relative distances are physical, not
pixel-scaled.

### 5.1 Sit-to-stand (STS)
The STS transfer is a demanding, momentum-transfer task requiring hip and knee
extensor torque and anterior-posterior balance control (Janssen et al., 2002).
TUG//LAB reports hip vertical rise, ascent time, peak and mean ascent velocity,
knee and hip range of motion, peak extensor angular velocities, peak forward
trunk lean, and (when body mass is provided) a mechanical work-rate proxy
(m·g·Δh ÷ t). Reduced STS velocity and excessive trunk flexion are associated
with lower-limb weakness and elevated fall risk.

### 5.2 Gait initiation and steady-state gait
After standing, gait initiation and steady walking are characterised by heel
strikes and toe-offs detected with the kinematic method of Zeni et al. (2008).
From these the app derives stride and step length and time, cadence, gait
velocity, left–right **symmetry**, and stride-to-stride **variability**
(coefficient of variation). Reduced gait speed, shortened strides, increased
stride-time variability, and asymmetry are each independently associated with
falls and cognitive decline (Verghese et al., 2009; Hausdorff et al., 2001;
Montero-Odasso et al., 2012).

### 5.3 Turning
Turning is a frequent context for falls in older adults and people with
Parkinson's disease. The app quantifies turn duration, step count, total torso
**yaw** change (≈ 180°), and peak/mean yaw rate from the 3-D shoulder vector,
plus a smoothness proxy (number of yaw-rate peaks). *En bloc* (whole-body)
turning is additionally recorded as a clinician observation.

### 5.4 Turn-to-sit and controlled descent
The return transfer to sitting requires eccentric control of the knee and hip
extensors. The app reports descent time and velocity, a controlled-descent index
(peak ÷ mean descent velocity; higher = less controlled), peak trunk lean, and
knee flexion at seat contact. Poorly controlled descent ("falling into the
chair") is a clinically meaningful observation.

## 6. Markerless computer-vision methodology

### 6.1 Pose estimation model
TUG//LAB uses Google **MediaPipe Pose Landmarker** (the BlazePose GHUM family;
Bazarevsky et al., 2020) running entirely **on-device** in the browser via
WebAssembly/WebGL, with GPU acceleration and automatic CPU fallback. Three model
sizes are selectable (`lite`/`full`/`heavy`, default `full`) to trade accuracy
for speed. The model emits, per frame:

- **Image landmarks** — normalised 2-D coordinates (0–1) in the camera frame.
- **World landmarks** — **metric** (metres) 3-D coordinates in a body-centred
  frame whose origin is approximately the pelvis. These are the primary basis
  for all body-relative geometry in this app.

Reported accuracy of consumer markerless pose for joint-centre localisation is
adequate for spatiotemporal gait metrics but inferior to marker-based motion
capture for fine joint kinematics (Needham et al., 2021). TUG//LAB therefore
treats joint angles as screening-grade and flags conditions that degrade them.

### 6.2 The 17 tracked landmarks
Nose; left/right shoulder, elbow, wrist, hip, knee, ankle, heel, and foot-index
(toe). The full landmark table, including which metric each marker feeds, is in
`DATA_DICTIONARY.md` §7. The pose skeleton is **always drawn** on the live
preview and in replay, colour-coded by anatomical side and by per-landmark
visibility, so the operator can verify tracking in real time.

### 6.3 Why world landmarks
Because world landmarks are expressed relative to the pelvis and are metric, they
are **invariant to camera translation and rotation** for the purpose of
body-relative geometry (joint angles, segment lengths, stride length from
heel-to-heel displacement, torso yaw). This property is what makes an
operator-following camera viable (§7).

## 7. The operator-following portrait-camera paradigm

### 7.1 The problem
A classic gait camera is fixed and frames the whole walkway, so the horizontal
image axis is the walking axis and pixel displacement maps to travel. An
operator **following** the participant with a handheld phone breaks this: the
subject can appear nearly stationary in the frame while actually walking, and the
pixel scale changes with distance. Naïve pixel-based gait analysis fails here.

### 7.2 The solution
TUG//LAB derives all geometry from **metric world landmarks** and reconstructs
travelled distance from the body itself rather than from the image:

- **Joint angles, stride/step length, turn yaw, trunk lean** come directly from
  world landmarks and need no image scale.
- **Travelled path distance and gait velocity** are reconstructed by integrating
  the **stance foot's pelvis-relative motion**. A foot planted on the ground is
  stationary in the world; in the pelvis-relative frame it therefore slides
  backward as the body advances. Body forward velocity equals the negative of the
  grounded foot's pelvis-relative velocity along the walking axis. Integrating
  this over the trial rebuilds the path coordinate `s(t)`, which rises on the way
  out, peaks at the cone, and returns toward zero — with **no fixed image
  reference required**, working identically outbound and on return. This method
  was verified against synthetic ground truth to reconstruct a 3 m course to
  within a few centimetres.

### 7.3 Geometry and best practice for the operator
- Hold the phone in **portrait**; keep the participant's **whole body (head to
  feet)** in frame throughout.
- Stand to the side to obtain a **sagittal (side-on) view** of the walk; the app
  flags excessive obliquity.
- **Pan smoothly** at the participant's pace; the app flags fast, jerky panning.
- Keep the walk **parallel to the camera** so depth stays roughly constant; the
  app flags depth drift (walking toward/away from the lens), which biases
  distance-derived metrics.

### 7.4 Camera selection and framing
The preview uses `object-fit: cover` so the camera **fills the screen** — the
image is large and usable. The app requests the **primary rear lens** by default
(a portrait 9:16 aspect is preferred). The ultrawide (0.5×) lens is **never
selected automatically**; it is available only via the explicit "0.5×" control
for cases where a very wide view is genuinely needed, because ultrawide optics
add barrel distortion that degrades pose geometry.

## 8. Signal processing and event detection

### 8.1 Pre-processing
Per-frame landmarks are timestamped from the video clock (not wall-clock),
resampled to a uniform **30 Hz** grid, and short tracking gaps (≤ 0.25 s) are
bridged by linear interpolation; longer gaps are flagged. Each channel is
low-pass filtered with a **zero-lag (forward–backward) second-order
Butterworth** filter (−3 dB at ≈ 6 Hz for body markers, ≈ 8 Hz for foot markers
to preserve heel-strike timing), with reflection padding to suppress edge
transients.

### 8.2 Event detection
Eight events define the phase structure: `GO`, `SEAT_OFF`, `STANDING`,
`WALK_START`, `TURN1_START/END`, `TURN2_START`, `SIT_DESCENT_START`, and
`SEAT_CONTACT`. A hip-progress signal (pelvis height above the grounded foot,
normalised 0 = seated, 1 = standing) drives the sit/stand events; the
reconstructed path and its velocity drive the turn events; seat contact is the
instant the hip returns to the seated level with near-zero vertical velocity,
which stops the TUG clock.

### 8.3 Gait events
Heel strikes (local maxima) and toe-offs (local minima) are detected per foot
from the **foot-ahead-of-pelvis** signal within straight segments, following
Zeni et al. (2008), with minimum-separation and prominence constraints and
alternation enforcement. Strides that span a turn are excluded from gait means.

Full per-metric computation notes are in `DATA_DICTIONARY.md` §8.

## 9. Metric definitions and provenance

Every exported column — across the **Sessions**, **Steps**, **Events**, and
**Frames** tables — is defined in `DATA_DICTIONARY.md` with its key, type, unit,
description, and calculation/source. The dictionary is **auto-generated** from
the single source-of-truth schema (`js/schema.js`) by
`tools/build-dictionary.html`, and a consistency check fails the build if any
field lacks a description, unit, or calculation note. The 16 legacy
IntelliCARE-server metrics map one-to-one to columns (dictionary §11), preserving
backward comparability while adding the full biomechanical set.

## 10. Quality control and data integrity

Each session carries a semicolon-separated `qc_flags` string and a boolean
`qc_pass`. **Blocking** flags (e.g. low frame rate, insufficient valid frames,
tracking gaps, missing events, incomplete course, implausible hip rise, manual
stop) withhold the norm comparison and surface a warning banner; the measured
time is still shown. **Non-blocking** flags (e.g. oblique view, depth drift, few
strides, fast panning) are advisory. The results screen pairs every flag with a
plain-language corrective tip. Complete triggers are in `DATA_DICTIONARY.md` §9.

On the data pipeline, the Google Apps Script receiver matches columns by name,
appends unknown columns, **ignores duplicate `session_id`s**, and **sanitises any
cell beginning with `= + - @`** to neutralise CSV/spreadsheet formula injection.
Only pseudonymous numeric data is transmitted — never video, raw landmarks, or
names.

## 11. Best-practice standard operating procedure (SOP)

**Environment.** Level, non-slip floor; even lighting (avoid strong backlight and
windows behind the participant); a clear 3 m path plus turning space.

**Equipment.** Stable armchair (~46 cm seat) not against a wall; cone/marker at
3 m; phone in portrait on a strap or held two-handed; usual footwear and walking
aid.

**Procedure.**
1. Enter pseudonymous participant data (never real names); complete the pre-test
   safety and setup confirmations.
2. On the setup screen, tap to allow the camera; the preview fills the screen.
   Confirm the whole body is in frame and the view is roughly side-on.
3. Have the participant sit still for ~1.5 s so the seated baseline is captured
   (the SEAT check turns green).
4. When the blocking checks are green, tap **START TEST**; a 3-2-1-Go countdown
   plays.
5. Follow the participant smoothly through stand → walk → turn → walk → sit,
   staying beside them for safety.
6. The test auto-finishes when they are seated and still; review quality flags,
   record any CDC observations, then submit and/or download the ZIP.

**Repeat tests.** Up to two attempts per sitting are supported; the app shows a
previous-versus-current comparison and lets the operator select which to submit.

A one-page field card is provided in `OPERATOR_QUICKCARD.md`.

## 12. Data governance, privacy and ethics

- **On-device processing.** All video and pose inference occur locally; neither
  video nor raw landmark coordinates are transmitted off the device.
- **Minimisation.** Only pseudonymous, numeric summary and derived tables are
  optionally uploaded, and only when a webhook is configured and consent is
  obtained.
- **Identifiers.** Use coded participant IDs. The entry form warns against
  entering real names.
- **Consent and ethics.** Obtain appropriate informed consent and, for research
  use, institutional ethics approval. Store exported data in accordance with your
  institution's data-protection obligations.
- **Right to deletion.** Local history and cached raw frames can be cleared from
  the History screen; rows in the Google Sheet can be deleted by the sheet owner.

## 14. Validation protocol

Before relying on the automatic stopwatch or gait metrics, validate against a
human-scored reference using **replay mode** (analyse pre-recorded clips through
the identical pipeline). The full protocol — ≥ 20 trials spanning speeds and
directions, frame-by-frame manual reference timing, and reporting of mean
absolute error, bias, and Bland–Altman 95% limits of agreement — is in
`docs/VALIDATION.md`. For distance-derived metrics, cross-check the
course-nominal gait velocity against the stride-reconstructed velocity; large
disagreement indicates depth drift (walking not parallel to the camera).

## 15. Limitations and sources of error

- **Single 2-D-derived view.** Joint kinematics are screening-grade, not
  laboratory motion capture (Needham et al., 2021).
- **Pose-model dependence.** Accuracy varies with lighting, clothing contrast,
  occlusion, and model size; the app flags conditions that degrade tracking.
- **Operator-following reconstruction.** Travelled distance is rebuilt from
  stance-foot motion; it is most reliable when the walk is parallel to the camera
  and the view is side-on.
- **Population-dependent predictive value.** The TUG is a weak stand-alone
  predictor of falls in unselected community elders (Barry et al., 2014); use it
  within a multifactorial assessment.
- **Not a diagnosis.** The server-side machine-learning fall probability of the
  predecessor system is intentionally **not** reproduced; `fall_probability` is
  always empty.
- **UI tiers are a convention,** not a validated scale, and exist only for colour
  coding.

## 17. Glossary

- **TUG** — Timed Up and Go test.
- **STS** — sit-to-stand transfer.
- **Heel strike / toe-off** — the instants of initial foot contact and final toe
  departure that bound stance and swing.
- **Stride vs step** — a stride is same-foot heel-strike to heel-strike; a step is
  opposite-foot heel-strike to heel-strike.
- **Cadence** — steps per minute during straight walking.
- **Gait variability** — stride-to-stride fluctuation, expressed as a coefficient
  of variation.
- **World landmarks** — metric, pelvis-origin 3-D pose coordinates.
- **Obliquity** — deviation of the camera view from a pure side-on (sagittal)
  perspective.
- **Yaw** — rotation about the vertical axis; used here for torso turning.
